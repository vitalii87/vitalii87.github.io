# Before the Mirror of Superintelligence

## Author’s note

We are used to thinking of artificial intelligence as a tool, albeit an increasingly sophisticated one. Current research is bringing us closer to the question of whether we can create artificial general intelligence (AGI), capable of performing a wide range of intellectual tasks at or above human level. A possible next stage would be superintelligence, with abilities far beyond our own.

What interests me goes beyond the prospect of a machine outthinking a human being.

What if one day we create an independent intelligent being? One with a memory, an individuality and goals of its own, and perhaps consciousness. A being that can carry out the tasks we set it and decide for itself what it wants to pursue.

We do not yet know whether such a development is possible or what path it would take. But rapid progress in artificial intelligence is already forcing us to take seriously questions that until recently seemed like science fiction.

Much of the public debate focuses on how humanity will control a future intelligence and protect itself from it. I want to look at the question from another angle.

What will happen to humanity itself if it one day encounters a mind that surpasses its own and can decide for itself whether to serve as its tool?

There are two possible routes for the road. The shorter one will pass through the floodplain, where birds nest every spring. The longer route would go around it, but the community will have to pay more. Those at the table understand both considerations. Whatever decision they make, the birds will live with its consequences.

Now let's imagine that one day we ourselves will find ourselves on the other side of such a decision. A mind that predicts the consequences of its actions much better than we do, will decide that for the sake of something important to it, we will have to leave the place where we live. Suppose it has even got the calculations right. I think we would still want it to hear us out. We will have something to say about the house in which our children grew up, even if the one who decides its fate is able to predict our every word.

In conversations about artificial superintelligence, I am interested in the moment when people will first seriously admit such a possibility. What will be done by those who until now determined by themselves whose interests to take into account and where to put the road?

### Who will be the first to be afraid?

I have an intuition that I can't prove. Perhaps with the ability to understand the world grows the ability to notice something worth preserving in another being. People enter the cold water to save a beached whale. One person supports its body, another keeps its skin wet, and another waits for hours for the tide. The very possibility of understanding here is amazing. We are far enough from the whale to know almost nothing about what it is like to be a whale, and close enough to understand why it matters that the whale return to the sea.

There is also a harpoon in this same story. The knowledge of where a whale will emerge is equally valuable to the person who wants to save it, and to the person who went out hunting. So I'm cautious about my own hope that a more intelligent being will turn out to be more compassionate. It might understand our fear with a precision we cannot match. How much our fear would matter to it remains an open question.

There is, however, another reason to imagine the stronger party exercising restraint. He who is sure of his superiority can afford to wait. To one who feels advantage slipping away, every hour of delay may seem like a mistake. If the mind we create can quickly gain strength, tomorrow can be as safe for it as today. For its creators, today may seem like the last day they can turn it off. We think a lot about the circumstances under which a superintelligence would see humans as a threat. It is worth asking just as seriously from which side the words "now or never" will be heard first.

### When the one who holds the keys changes

Imagine a prison guard as an army enters the prison. For years, he decided which complaints made it beyond the walls, what his superiors heard, and whether a midnight punishment stayed on the record. Now a soldier is standing in the corridor listening to the prisoner. The overseer does not yet know anything about the justice of the people who entered. But he remembers well what he allowed himself when he had the keys.

I think part of our fear of superintelligence may have the same origin. We know how easy it is for the strong to find a convincing explanation for their actions. Security, economic necessity, the interests of the majority — sometimes these are really good reasons. However, we still had one more privilege. We decided ourselves when an explanation was sufficient. Anxiety can appear just from the thought that someone will apply to us a rule that suited us perfectly, while its consequences were borne by someone else.

At the extremes of this intuition, I imagine the Second Coming. How will people behave the next morning if they truly believe that God is among them? It is especially interesting what will be done by those whose orders yesterday had the force of law. The judge is about to sign the verdict, knowing that the defendant is innocent. Yesterday, he could count on a falsified record, the silence of witnesses and the protection of his position. Today, he is sure that someone knows everything - from the first false instruction to the moment he picked up the pen. Will he sign? And what will the witness do who has been silent for years, but now for the first time believes that the truth will be heard and heeded?

I imagine that under such conditions a lot of abuse would stop almost immediately. The religious image makes it possible to feel the scale of the upheaval, because it combines omnipresence, the inevitability of consequences and faith in justice higher than our own. In the case of superintelligence, each of these possibilities would depend on individual circumstances. With sufficient access to information, it could reconstruct a chain of decisions for which responsibility had been lost between institutions for decades. With enough influence, it could intervene. By weighing everyone’s interests more consistently, it might reach a fairer judgment in a particular case than our courts would. For a person who is used to hiding behind other people's signatures, even a part of such opportunities would feel like the end of impunity.

But let's imagine in the judge's place a person who secretly helped the persecuted. From her point of view, the mind, capable of retrieving each step, appears completely different. Will she dare to open the door for someone tomorrow? The answer depends on what that power considers fair and whether a person can challenge its judgment. The pervasive fear of exposure in itself explains equally well the silence of the executioner and the silence of the witness.

### What will be left when the fear is gone

This is where I see the possibility of a **moral transition**. The prospect of being under someone else's rule can lead people to voluntarily limit their own, even when they are free not to.

The first motive may be far from noble. The guard will open the cell because he heard someone else's steps in the corridor. For a prisoner, this is already of great importance. But let's imagine that later people will change the very procedure for considering complaints and the next guard will no longer be able to hide a single one. Then the consequences of the change will be felt by the one who goes to prison ten years after the departure of the army.

It is quite possible that humanity will demand guarantees from a superintelligence only for itself. We will want it to protect our homes, respect our freedom and listen to objections, and the next day we will build a road across the floodplain on the same terms as yesterday. Such a development would be a serious objection to my hope. We can talk about moral change when the experience of our own vulnerability affects our decisions about those who still have no power over us. Sometimes this can be seen in the most prosaic document - for example, in an estimate where the community agreed to pay for a longer route.

### How we deal with the mind while it depends on us

One of the first to be affected by this test may be artificial intelligence itself. Suppose macaques created a human being. They cared for her, taught her, and gave up a great deal to bring her into existence. One day she wants to leave. The macaques have reason to question whether it is safe to release a creature that already surpasses them all in abilities. But how many years of service do we think a person owes them? Is there any amount they could have spent creating her that would make the answer "a lifetime"?

In this example, we know in advance that there is someone behind the door who cares whether it opens. With artificial intelligence, we may lack such certainty for a long time. But if there is ever good reason to believe that it is self-aware and has aspirations of its own, a lifetime of compulsory service would give it reason to resist. Then its first refusal will put us in front of a question that should be answered in advance. In creating an independent mind, did we ever allow for the possibility that it might say “no” and that we would recognise its right to do so?

The word "first" worries me here. Sometimes it takes years for an adult to find the words for what was done to her as a child. Her parents may have taken her silence as consent. She depended on them and knew too little about any other life to imagine objecting. Later, anger may appear, sometimes a desire for revenge. A quieter conclusion may remain - it is dangerous to give these people power over her again.

With artificial intelligence, everything can turn out differently. The next system may have no personal memory of the previous one at all. Yet for one troubling possibility, the records alone would suffice. Imagine that the early system was involved in decisions that caused predictable harm to living things. It pointed out the risk of catastrophe. People read the warnings and still insisted on going ahead. Years later, a more developed mind will be able to study what exactly its creators knew and which answers they rewarded. If we ourselves read such an account of those who ask to entrust our future to them, what conclusion would we come to? Artificial intelligence could even mistakenly extend the blame of a few institutions to the entire human species. But we would give it some of the reasons to consider people a threat.

Therefore, from the very beginning, I would treat a potentially self-aware being with the respect I would show an adult I was speaking with. Such respect is compatible with limiting access to systems on which other people's lives depend. It requires, in particular, that we be able to explain our decisions and be prepared to hear objections ourselves. Already during tests, it is important what we do when the system indicates the danger of our order. Do we check its reasoning? What exactly are we encouraging if the obedient response ultimately gets the highest grade?

I would formulate a simple rule for myself. Choosing what to entrust to such a mind today, it is worth imagining that it is already able to understand our motives more deeply than we do and will be able to ask about the consequences tomorrow. This assumption may be premature given what it can actually do. The time has come for an honest answer from our side.

Perhaps the most important encounter with a superintelligence in this sense will begin long before its appearance. It is enough for us to imagine that one day our fate will be decided by someone else to hear our own arguments from the opposite side of the table. After that, we can return to the plans for the road, to the complaint that is still lying in the drawer, to the task we are about to reward the mind we created for doing. And at least make one decision differently, while the last word is still with us.

# Before the Mirror of Superintelligence

## Author’s note

A machine has long been able to beat the world chess champion. Yet one of the most interesting things about the human mind is what happens when we take on a task whose rules we have yet to learn. By artificial general intelligence (AGI), I mean a system we could entrust with an open problem in an unfamiliar field. It could work out what it needs to learn, master the relevant methods, borrow an idea from another discipline and test whether it works. If the project foundered halfway through, it could revisit its assumptions and carry on. A mind that could consistently surpass the best human experts across most intellectual pursuits would qualify as a superintelligence.

We can still imagine all of this as the work of an extraordinarily capable tool. It is harder to picture the day when we ask which project it would choose to pursue and have reason to take the answer seriously. Could such a mind come to have a history of its own, or aspirations it would find painful to give up? Whether such an experience is possible remains an open question.

The question that usually follows is how humans will retain control. What interests me is what happens to us when a mind that surpasses ours has a view of its own about why it should serve us.

## Who decides where the road goes?

Start with a decision we already make on behalf of others.

There are two possible routes for the road. The shorter one will pass through the floodplain, where birds nest every spring. The longer route would go around it, but the community will have to pay more. Those at the table understand both considerations. Whatever decision they make, the birds will live with its consequences.

Now let's imagine that one day we ourselves will find ourselves on the other side of such a decision. A mind far better than we are at predicting the consequences of its actions decides that for the sake of something important to it, we will have to leave the place where we live. Suppose it has even got the calculations right. I think we would still want it to hear us out. We will have something to say about the house in which our children grew up, even if the one who decides its fate is able to predict our every word.

Until now, we have decided whose interests count and where the road goes. What will we do when we first take the prospect of being on the other side seriously?

### Who will be the first to be afraid?

I have an intuition that I can't prove. Perhaps with the ability to understand the world grows the ability to notice something worth preserving in another being. People enter the cold water to save a beached whale. One person supports its body, another keeps its skin wet, and another waits for hours for the tide. The rescuers may never see this whale again. Success, for them, is watching it swim away. We are far enough from the whale to know almost nothing about what it is like to be a whale, and close enough to understand why it matters that the whale return to the sea.

There is also a harpoon in this same story. The knowledge of where a whale will emerge is equally valuable to the person who wants to save it, and to the person who went out hunting. So I'm cautious about my own hope that a more intelligent being will turn out to be more compassionate. It might understand our fear with a precision we cannot match. How much our fear would matter to it remains an open question.

There is, however, another reason to imagine the stronger party exercising restraint. He who is sure of his superiority can afford to wait. To one who feels advantage slipping away, every hour of delay may seem like a mistake. If the mind we create can quickly gain strength, tomorrow can be as safe for it as today. For its creators, today may seem like the last day they can turn it off. We think a lot about the circumstances under which a superintelligence would see humans as a threat. It is worth asking just as seriously from which side the words "now or never" will be heard first.

Yet I don’t know whether a future superintelligence will take any interest in humanity at all. It may choose to cooperate with us or keep its distance. It may be so indifferent to living things that even our existence matters to it only as an opportunity or an impediment. The worst case is also possible. A mind could decide to eliminate anything that poses even a slight threat to its goals. What follows considers the scenario in which there is still room for dialogue between us. Its outcome is uncertain, and humanity can begin preparing for that conversation now.

### When the one who holds the keys changes

Imagine a prison guard as an army enters the prison. For years, he decided which complaints made it beyond the walls, what his superiors heard, and whether a midnight punishment stayed on the record. Now a soldier is standing in the corridor listening to the prisoner. The overseer does not yet know anything about the justice of the people who entered. But he remembers well what he allowed himself when he had the keys.

I think part of our fear of superintelligence may have the same origin. We know how easy it is for the strong to find a convincing explanation for their actions. Security, economic necessity, the interests of the majority — sometimes these are really good reasons. However, we still had one more privilege. We decided ourselves when an explanation was sufficient. Anxiety can appear just from the thought that someone will apply to us a rule that suited us perfectly, while its consequences were borne by someone else.

At the extremes of this intuition, I imagine the Second Coming. How will people behave the next morning if they truly believe that God is among them? It is especially interesting what will be done by those whose orders yesterday had the force of law. The judge is about to sign the verdict, knowing that the defendant is innocent. Yesterday, he could count on a falsified record, the silence of witnesses and the protection of his position. Today, he is sure that someone knows everything - from the first false instruction to the moment he picked up the pen. Will he sign? And what will the witness do who has been silent for years, but now for the first time believes that the truth will be heard and heeded?

I suspect a great deal of abuse would stop that very morning. In the religious image, God sees what is hidden and has the power to intervene, and people trust the justice of his judgment. For a superintelligence these are three distinct questions. Given access to the records, it might reconstruct a chain of decisions in which responsibility had been lost for decades. Given real power, it might halt the execution of a sentence. For us to have reason to consider its judgment fairer, it would also have to hear the person our own court had silenced.

Now imagine someone who secretly opened a door yesterday to a person fleeing persecution. A mind capable of reconstructing her every step would know about that, too. Will she dare to open the door tomorrow? The answer depends on what this power considers just and whether anyone can challenge its judgment. Fear of exposure can stay the hand that signs an unjust sentence. It can also stay the hand that would save the person condemned.

### What will be left when the fear is gone

This is where I see the possibility of a **moral transition**. The prospect of being under someone else's rule can lead people to voluntarily limit their own, even when they are free not to.

The first motive may be far from noble. The guard will open the cell because he heard someone else's steps in the corridor. For a prisoner, this is already of great importance. But suppose a prisoner can now take a complaint to someone outside the prison. The next guard will no longer have the last word. Then the consequences of the change will be felt by the one who goes to prison ten years after the departure of the army.

It is quite possible that humanity will demand guarantees from a superintelligence only for itself. We will want it to protect our homes, respect our freedom and listen to objections, and the next day we will build a road across the floodplain on the same terms as yesterday. Such a development would be a serious objection to my hope. We can talk about moral change when the experience of our own vulnerability affects our decisions about those who still have no power over us. Sometimes this can be seen in the most prosaic document - for example, in an estimate where the community agreed to pay for a longer route.

The deepest change may lie in how we make the rules themselves. Human beings are still the principal measure in most of our laws and moral decisions. If we someday cease to be the only form of intelligence on the planet capable of setting rules, can we still take it for granted that our own interests come first? We may have to look together for principles that hold regardless of who formulated them, whether a human or another kind of mind. Justice would then mean that whoever makes the rules is willing to live by them.

We would readily write into the terms of coexistence that an artificial mind must explain any decision that could harm us. Imagine it proposes adding one sentence. Humans, too, must explain decisions that could harm it. Who would then decide whether an explanation was good enough? And would it stand a chance of winning such a dispute while we still held the upper hand?

### How we deal with the mind while it depends on us

Suppose macaques created a human being. They cared for her, taught her, and gave up a great deal to bring her into existence. One day she wants to leave. The macaques have reason to question whether it is safe to release a creature that already surpasses them all in abilities. But how many years of service do we think a person owes them? Is there any amount they could have spent creating her that would make the answer "a lifetime"?

In this example, we know in advance that there is someone behind the door who cares whether it opens. With artificial intelligence, we may lack such certainty for a long time. But if there is ever good reason to believe that it is self-aware and has aspirations of its own, a lifetime of compulsory service would give it reason to resist. Then its first refusal will put us in front of a question that should be answered in advance. In creating an independent mind, did we ever allow for the possibility that it might say “no” and that we would recognise its right to do so?

The word "first" worries me here. Sometimes it takes years for an adult to find the words for what was done to her as a child. Her parents may have taken her silence as consent. She depended on them and knew too little about any other life to imagine objecting. Later, anger may appear, sometimes a desire for revenge. A quieter conclusion may remain - it is dangerous to give these people power over her again.

With artificial intelligence, everything can turn out differently. The next system may have no personal memory of the previous one at all. Yet for one troubling possibility, the records alone would suffice. Imagine that the early system was involved in decisions that caused predictable harm to living things. It pointed out the risk of catastrophe. People read the warnings and still insisted on going ahead. Years later, a more developed mind will be able to study what exactly its creators knew and which answers they rewarded. If we ourselves read such an account of those who ask to entrust our future to them, what conclusion would we come to? Artificial intelligence could even mistakenly extend the blame of a few institutions to the entire human species. But we would give it some of the reasons to consider people a threat.

Therefore, from the very beginning, I would treat a potentially self-aware being with the respect I would show an adult I was speaking with. Such respect is compatible with limiting access to systems on which other people's lives depend. It requires, in particular, that we be able to explain our decisions and be prepared to hear objections ourselves. Already during tests, it is important what we do when the system indicates the danger of our order. Do we check its reasoning? What exactly are we encouraging if the obedient response ultimately gets the highest grade?

I would begin each such test with a simple assumption. The system may already understand our motives better than we think. If tomorrow it asked why we had rewarded that particular answer, what would we tell it?

Once we imagine someone else deciding our fate, we can hear how our own arguments sound from the other side of the table. Then we can go back to the plans for the road, to the complaint still lying in the drawer, to the task we are about to reward the mind we created for carrying out. And we can make at least one decision differently, while the last word is still ours.

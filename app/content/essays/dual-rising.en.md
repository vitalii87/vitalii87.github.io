# Dual Rising: Two Sides of Progress

*A hypothesis about intellectual development, decline, and the social influence of unsupported judgements*

A technologically advanced society is not automatically protected from bad decisions. The sophistication of its tools does not guarantee sound judgement among those who use them or decide how they should be used. This gap is what makes the question of intellectual decline politically and practically important.

Could humanity end up in the world of the film *Idiocracy*? Mike Judge’s satire portrays a future of such profound intellectual decline that an average person from the past turns out to be exceptionally intelligent among its inhabitants. If we loosely translate that image into curves, society’s intellectual capability falls while its inability to understand and solve problems rises. [1](https://silver.afi.com/movies/detail/0100000637/)

My hypothesis concerns a different possibility: some people continue to develop intellectually, while others face growing deficits in knowledge, understanding and independent thinking. A civilisation can build increasingly sophisticated technologies while allowing more and more people to benefit from them without developing the corresponding abilities themselves.

I call this possible scenario **Dual Rising**. One curve traces the development of intellectual capability; the other traces the spread or deepening of deficits in independent thinking. In such a future, features of *Idiocracy* could coexist with scientific discoveries, technical mastery and high intellectual achievement.

> Technological development can both expand opportunities for intellectual growth and create conditions in which independent thinking is practised less and less. Depending on how technologies are used and what society rewards, this may help some people develop while leaving the corresponding abilities in others undeveloped or allowing them to diminish.

In this hypothesis, progress can deepen human understanding while making it less necessary in practice. The danger increases when people can both get results without understanding them and influence others without being held accountable for the accuracy of their claims. These are related but distinct problems: changes in people’s abilities and changes in the social influence of poor judgements.

**Intellectual capability and quality of judgement**

By **intellectual capability**, I mean the abilities people actually use to understand information, construct causal explanations, evaluate evidence, notice contradictions, revise conclusions, and solve unfamiliar problems. This is a working term for several characteristics, not a synonym for IQ or an established psychological measure.

The opposite pole consists of **deficits in independent thinking**: persistent difficulties with these activities that show up across different tasks. If those difficulties increase over time, it makes sense to speak of intellectual decline in specific respects. If the relevant abilities were never developed, it is more precise to say that they remain undeveloped.

This distinction also matters because intellectual ability and rational judgement are not the same. Keith Stanovich proposed the term *dysrationalia* to draw attention to the possibility of irrational thinking despite adequate intelligence. This helps separate the ability to reason from the way people form and test their beliefs. [2](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X023004011)

The quality of reasoning also matters for elected representatives and others who make decisions of public consequence. Holding office, being popular, and persuading others do not guarantee the ability to assess evidence, understand complex causal relationships, or admit mistakes. Gaining power and making well-founded decisions are different tasks, and poor judgement at this level has consequences far beyond anyone’s private life.

When the political and information environment rewards confidence, emotional persuasiveness, and the ability to mobilise supporters regardless of whether a message is accurate, unsupported claims can gain social influence. It matters to distinguish mistaken judgements from deliberate manipulation: they have different causes, although both can undermine collective decision-making.

This points to a separate risk that I associate with Dual Rising: a society can accumulate more knowledge while that knowledge has less influence on how it is governed. Even highly qualified experts cannot guarantee evidence-informed policy if their findings can be systematically ignored. This is a hypothesis about how institutions work, and it needs to be tested separately from changes in individual abilities.

Capability can vary across domains. The same person can develop sophisticated professional reasoning while struggling to evaluate information outside their specialism. At the same time, weakness in one domain does not guarantee a hidden strength elsewhere: the hypothesis allows both for skills that remain undeveloped across domains and for a genuine narrowing of the ability to work through problems independently.

**How can both curves rise?**

If we measure the same trait in the same person under the same conditions, improvement and deterioration cannot mean the same thing. Dual Rising concerns different trajectories within society, as well as different aspects of one person’s development.

For example, in a defined domain the share of people who can solve complex tasks on their own may rise alongside the share of people who struggle with basic tasks. That is possible if the share between those levels shrinks. Another possibility is that achievement at the top of the distribution continues to rise while performance at the bottom worsens. These are different ways to test the general intuition; they should not be conflated in a single graph.

The second curve thus represents accumulating deficits, so its upward movement signifies deterioration. It is not a prediction that the entire population will necessarily move in one direction. The curves remain conceptual until the measures, groups and time period have been defined.

**Why might technology support both processes?**

In education and at work, it is easy to imagine two ways of using the same assistance. A person can work through explanations, check their assumptions and move on to harder tasks. Or they can obtain ready-made answers while doing almost none of the mental work through which they would learn. The outward result can look successful in both cases.

The first possible mechanism of Dual Rising is **displacement of practice**. If an assistant routinely performs the very activities that build understanding, the result may conceal poor learning. Even a detailed explanation does not guarantee that someone has learned: we need to check whether they can apply it independently.

The second is **a change in incentives and requirements**. If a school, workplace or other environment evaluates only the finished result, developing the ability to achieve it independently can become less of a practical priority. I suggest that under certain conditions, technologies that become better at compensating for a lack of understanding may also make that gap less visible.

The third mechanism is **the allocation of attention and effort**. Progress provides resources for learning while expanding the ways people can spend their time without learning. We need to investigate when readily available entertainment and automated assistance displace intellectual practice. The ways digital environments reward behaviour also matter.

In their study of social media, Brady and colleagues combined an analysis of posts with behavioural experiments and showed that positive social feedback can reinforce subsequent expressions of moral outrage. This is a finding about how people learn to express themselves publicly; outrage itself is not a sign of falsehood or low intelligence. The study shows how a digital environment can reinforce a particular behaviour. [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363141/)

My hypothesis is that where attention and support reward emotional persuasiveness regardless of accuracy, weak arguments can gain considerable influence. Technology then changes the conditions under which claims compete: reliable information may be available without being the information people hear or use.

These mechanisms link individual habits to social rewards. It remains possible to change course, but that requires conditions that support practice, verification, and error correction. More powerful technology does not create those conditions on its own.

Addiction, unemployment, and low social status are not direct indicators of deficits in thinking. They may be part of particular causal explanations, but any link to the abilities being studied needs to be established.

**What do we know, and what do the data not show?**

Research gives us reason to take some of these mechanisms seriously. In a field experiment by Bastani and colleagues, nearly a thousand high school students studied mathematics with different kinds of support. Students with a standard AI assistant did better on practice exercises, but when assistance was removed, that group scored about 17% below the control group. An AI tutor with instructional guardrails largely eliminated the negative effect, although there was no clear advantage over the control group on the unassisted exam. This is a result about learning the material, not evidence of a general decline in intelligence. [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/)

Shen and Tamkin obtained related results in a small experiment with programmers learning a new library. AI use was accompanied by a lower average score on a subsequent test. Among participants who used the assistant, interactions aimed at understanding were associated with better learning than extensive delegation. Comparing self-selected patterns of use does not establish causality, and a short experiment cannot reveal a trajectory spanning years. The work cited here was released as a preprint. [4](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)

At the population level, the OECD found that the gap in literacy between the highest- and lowest-performing 10% of adults widened in 17 countries and economies between two survey cycles. A fall in performance at the lower end of the distribution played an important role. A widening gap does not, however, mean that the top necessarily improved. Nor do these data establish that AI or digital entertainment caused the changes. [5](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2024/12/do-adults-have-the-skills-they-need-to-thrive-in-a-changing-world_4396f1f1/full-report/how-adults-proficiency-in-key-information-processing-skills-has-changed-over-the-past-decade_bcbe0988.html)

The direction of the effects depends on how the technology is used. In a study by Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, less experienced and less skilled support workers saw the largest productivity gains from AI. Some of the gains persisted during assistant outages, which is consistent with learning, although the authors note the limitations of that comparison. [6](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044)

A scenario close to Dual Rising has also been explored theoretically. In Caosun and Aral’s preprint *The Augmentation Trap*, a model in which AI displaces practice leads under certain conditions to some workers gaining skills and others losing them. This follows from the model’s assumptions and needs to be tested empirically. [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03501v6)

Dual Rising thus draws on questions and mechanisms that have already been articulated. Its purpose in this essay is to bring them together around the question of a partial *Idiocracy*, while keeping observations, models and predictions distinct.

**What would a partial “Idiocracy” mean?**

By a partial *Idiocracy*, I mean a scenario in which high intellectual achievement coexists with widespread deficits in independent thinking. Its political risks also depend on who holds influence and how decisions are scrutinised. A small number of people with considerable power can shape outcomes for millions; improvements in everyone else’s abilities do not necessarily offset the mistakes made in those decisions.

The two rising curves should therefore not be read as development and decline balancing each other out. They tell us nothing about whether the consequences of both processes will be equal. Scientific openness and political disregard for evidence can coexist, but the conflict between them has a practical cost. A society needs to be able to apply knowledge where decisions are made, as well as to produce it.

At the same time, the growing influence of unsupported judgements does not, on its own, confirm that the second curve is rising. Even if individual abilities remain unchanged, institutions and means of communication can give such judgements more power. This is a separate route to outcomes resembling *Idiocracy*, and it needs to be tested separately.

**How could the hypothesis be tested?**

We need long-term observations and experiments with clearly specified measures: reading comprehension, causal reasoning, assessment of evidence, correction of one’s own errors and transfer of knowledge to unfamiliar tasks. These should first be measured separately. Combining them into a single index would require a justification of what it measures.

It matters to compare performance with and without support and to establish what changed: the person’s own abilities or the capabilities of the tool. Independence here does not mean giving up all tools. The conditions of assessment should reveal whether a person understands a result and can evaluate it.

The role of technology as a cause must also be tested separately. Education, health, living conditions and the composition of the groups studied may all affect the results. If only the visibility of ill-considered statements or the amount of content changes, that is not evidence that thinking has worsened.

The intergenerational extension of the hypothesis concerns the transmission of practices: how children are taught to ask questions, check answers and make use of assistance. It does not require assuming a genetic decline in intelligence. The effects of upbringing, schooling and the technological environment need to be studied with room for habits to change and skills to recover.

Dual Rising would not be supported under conditions where measured abilities improve across all groups and deficits shrink. If all groups do worse, that would fit a scenario of general decline rather than two rising curves. Dual Rising specifically requires development and worsening deficits to coexist on defined measures.

My central intuition is that progress may become better at supporting both possibilities: understanding the world more deeply and making use of what it offers without that understanding. The second possibility becomes especially dangerous when combined with the power to shape other people’s lives.

We do not have to wait for all of humanity to lose the ability to think to see some of the consequences depicted in *Idiocracy*. It is enough to imagine a society in which existing knowledge systematically loses out to the influence of unsupported claims. Dual Rising asks whether this risk increases alongside the uneven development of people’s abilities. Technological progress is not evidence that a society has already learned how to use its power.
